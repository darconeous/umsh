UMSH Protocol Specification

LAN Bearer

Note

This section is an early work in progress and this protocol may change significantly.

The LAN bearer carries UMSH frames between the participants on one local network, using IPv4 multicast. Repeaters on the network join their LoRa meshes to it, so that two meshes on different LoRa settings, or two radios out of range of each other, are joined by the network they share. Hosts with no radio of their own join it too, and reach every mesh a radio on the network carries.

A multicast group is used because every participant hears every other with no server, no configuration beyond the group, and no state about who else is present.

Participants

  • A bridging radio has a LoRa radio and a node whose repeater is enabled, and forwards between the two by the rules in Forwarding Across Bearers. A radio whose repeater is disabled sends only its own traffic and its host’s to the group, as an endpoint does.
  • An endpoint has a node and no LoRa radio. It sends its own traffic to the group and receives what is sent there. A desktop tool or a phone on the network is an endpoint.
  • A virtual radio is a ULCP device whose physical layer is the group: what it transmits is sent to the group, and what it receives is every datagram from the group, delivered to its host with no measurements. It has no node and no repeater of its own. Its host is an endpoint that reaches the group through it, exactly as a host reaches the air through a LoRa radio.

Transport

The bearer is UDP over IPv4 multicast. The default group is 239.255.134.121, in the administratively scoped range, and the default port is 15056. A deployment MAY configure another IPv4 multicast group and another nonzero port; every participant that is to hear the others uses the same pair.

Every participant sends from the group’s port as well as to it, and listens on it; several participants on one host share it.

A participant joins the group on the interface it uses for the local network and sends to the group from that interface, with a multicast TTL of 16 unless configured otherwise. How far the group reaches is the network’s decision: routers forward a multicast group only where they are configured to, and 239.255.0.0/16 is scoped to the organization. A network that routes the group between its subnets joins their participants into one bearer.

Point-to-Point

A unicast address MAY be configured in place of the group, naming the one peer of a point-to-point link. The participant joins nothing, sends to the peer at the group’s port, and drops every datagram whose source address and port are not the peer’s. Each end names the other, on the same port.

Echoes

A participant in a multicast group hears its own datagrams when multicast loopback, or an access point repeating a station’s multicast, hands them back. A participant MAY drop a datagram whose source address and port are its own, which on a host with several participants also drops the others’. A participant that is alone on its host MAY instead disable multicast loopback.

Datagram Format

A datagram carries exactly one frame, with no header. The frame is the packet as it would be transmitted over the air: UMSH frames and MeshCore v1 frames are told apart by the version bits in their first octet, as on LoRa.

  • A sender MUST NOT send a datagram that is empty or larger than 255 octets.
  • A receiver drops, with accounting, a datagram that is empty, larger than 255 octets, or whose version bits name no protocol it recognizes.
  • A participant that does not carry MeshCore v1 frames ignores them.

The sender of a frame is not identified in the datagram, and nothing about the datagram’s source address is carried onward.

A datagram is a bearer reception. It was heard by every participant on the network, and measured by none of them: it carries no RSSI or SNR, and the signal-quality thresholds do not apply to it.

Discovery

Participants advertise themselves with DNS-SD over multicast DNS, under the service type _umsh._udp. A bridging radio SHOULD advertise; other participants MAY.

  • The instance name is the participant’s device name.
  • The SRV port is the group’s port.
  • The TXT record carries the keys below.

A client ignores keys it does not recognize, treats a missing optional key as false, and ignores a record whose v it does not recognize. Discovery is only discovery: frames travel over the group whatever the records say, and a client learns from them which LoRa meshes the network reaches and on which settings, before it sends anything.

Forwarding Across Bearers

A node MAY have one or more bearers beside its radio: shared media, such as a LAN, on which every other participant hears what the node sends. A bearer works as backhaul does:

  • What arrives on a bearer is a reception. The node handles it as it handles a packet heard on its radio, recording which bearer it arrived on, and applies the forwarding procedure to it once. With its repeater enabled the node may repeat it on its radio; with its repeater disabled it forwards nothing, whatever the packet arrived on.
  • What the radio transmits goes to every bearer. Each frame the node’s radio puts on the air is sent to each bearer the node participates in: its own packets and acknowledgments, its repeats, and the transmissions of any other stack sharing the radio. A frame the radio does not transmit—a repeat abandoned to a better-placed repeater, or cancelled by an overheard acknowledgment—does not reach the bearer.

Enabling a bearer therefore makes the node a participant on it, not a forwarder: a node whose repeater is disabled sends only its own traffic, and its host’s, to the bearer.

A crossing between two bridging radios spends two flood hops: one at the radio that hears the packet and repeats it, which is what reaches the bearer, and one at the radio that receives it from the bearer and repeats it. A packet a bridging radio originates spends one. A repeat returns to the bearer it came from, where every participant has already seen the packet and suppresses it as a duplicate.

A bearer reception adds a Trace Signal entry recording that nothing was measured, as a point-to-point reception does.

The duty cycle a radio spends carrying bearer receptions counts against its limit like any other forward. Copies sent to a bearer spend no radio airtime.

Ingress Limit

A bridging radio MUST bound the rate at which it hands bearer receptions to its node, so that a busy network cannot spend the radio’s airtime faster than its operator allows. A token bucket of frames per minute is recommended. Receptions over the limit are dropped with accounting.

Channel Access

Every bridging radio on a network receives each datagram at once, so radios on the same LoRa channel would carry it onto the air together. Bearer receptions therefore contend, and only what is heard over the air says anything about the air.

  • Bearer receptions contend. A bearer reception carries no measurement to rank radios by. Its flood contention window is drawn uniformly from [W_min, W_max], and the jitter and D_ack are added as for any other forward. A packet received over a point-to-point link keeps its zero window.
  • Only the air defers. Only a copy received over the radio defers or abandons a pending forward. A copy received from a bearer or a point-to-point link says nothing about who has transmitted on the radio’s channel, and is a duplicate and nothing more.
  • A bearer copy confirms. A copy received from a bearer is a frame some radio transmitted, and satisfies forwarding confirmation as a copy overheard on the air does.
  • Ack cancellation is unchanged. An overheard acknowledgment cancels a matching pending forward whatever it arrived on: it proves delivery either way.

Two radios on one LoRa channel that both hear a bearer reception draw different windows. The first to transmit is overheard by the second, which defers as it would for any repeat it overheard.

Security Considerations

  • The bearer adds no protection and removes none. UMSH frames are authenticated, and encrypted where their payload type calls for it, before they reach any medium. What crosses the LAN is what crosses the air.
  • The bearer reaches as far as the network routes the group. A network that routes it between subnets has extended the bearer, and the radios on it, to everyone the routing reaches.
  • Anyone on the network can send to the group. A participant on the LAN can inject frames into every mesh a bridging radio on it carries, as anyone with a LoRa radio can inject frames into the mesh around them, at a higher offered rate. The ingress limit and the duty-cycle limit bound what that costs the air.
  • Anyone on the network hears the group. The LAN carries what the radios on it hear and transmit; a listener on the LAN learns what a LoRa receiver near each of those radios would.
  • DNS-SD records reveal a radio’s public key and LoRa settings, which its transmissions already reveal to anyone in range, to everyone on the network.
  • Bridging joins meshes. The cautions in Routing Overview § Bridging apply: a bridging radio puts every mesh’s flood traffic on the others’ airtime. Region policy and the flood hop budget are the tools for keeping that local.
