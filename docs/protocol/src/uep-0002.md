This UEP states why UMSH exists, what it is for, the principles that guide
its design, what it aims to do and not do, and the shape of the design. It is
the reference against which design decisions are weighed: when two approaches
both work, the one that better serves the principles here is the one UMSH
takes.

## Motivation {#motivation}

UMSH is a mesh protocol for LoRa built on strong cryptography and a clean
architecture. Few of its ideas are new. Cryptographic addressing, source
routing, and regions all appear in MeshCore, which inspired UMSH. What UMSH
contributes is a design that puts those ideas on a sound cryptographic
footing and keeps its layers separate, so that the protocol can grow without
the backward-incompatible changes that a weaker foundation eventually forces.

## Use Cases {#use-cases}

UMSH is for deployments where LoRa's range and low power consumption are
valuable, and where LoRa's constraints—low data rates, small frames, a shared
channel—make efficiency and cryptographic robustness matter.

- **Off-grid text communication**: chat, direct messages, and group
  conversations where there is no cellular coverage, such as hiking,
  expeditions, disaster response, and rural communities.
- **Emergency and disaster communications**: a mesh that needs no fixed
  infrastructure and degrades gracefully as nodes go offline.
- **IoT and sensor telemetry**: authenticated readings from battery-powered
  field devices, where per-packet overhead costs battery life and a tampered
  reading has real consequences.
- **Amateur radio**: modes that carry callsigns and leave traffic unencrypted,
  for legal operation on amateur frequencies.
- **Privacy-sensitive communication**: encrypted group unicast and multicast
  conceal who is talking to whom, for settings where traffic analysis is a
  concern.
- **Embedded and constrained devices**: compact encoding, single-frame
  packets, and no mandatory runtime state suit bare-metal microcontrollers
  with little RAM and no operating system.

## Guiding Principles {#principles}

When evaluating a design decision, prefer the option that best satisfies these
principles. Their order does not rank them.

### Single-Frame Design {#single-frame}

Every packet fits in a single LoRa frame. An operation that needs more data
than one frame can carry belongs at a higher layer.

### Confidentiality {#confidentiality}

Encryption and authentication are available for every kind of interaction.
Where concealing metadata is also needed, the protocol provides opt-in
mechanisms rather than requiring it everywhere.

### Robustness {#robustness}

Prefer constructions that fail gracefully over ones that fail
catastrophically. A slightly less efficient design that degrades safely under
adverse conditions is better than an efficient one that breaks badly.

### Tolerance of Loss and Disorder {#loss-and-disorder}

Assume packets are dropped, duplicated, and delivered out of order. No
operation requires synchronized state between two nodes that cannot recover
from a missed message.

### Brevity {#brevity}

Every byte costs airtime. Mandatory fields are as small as correctness allows,
and optional fields are absent when not needed.

### Practicality {#practicality}

A protocol has to be operable, not just correct. Prefer designs that make real
networks easier to understand and debug: readable packet traces, attributable
traffic, and identifiable nodes are operational requirements, not luxuries.
Theoretical minimalism that makes a protocol hard to deploy or troubleshoot is
a real cost.

### Layer Separation {#layer-separation}

The MAC layer routes and delivers opaque payloads. It does not depend on
payload content, and payload protocols do not depend on MAC-layer internals.

### Minimal Mandatory State {#minimal-state}

Basic operation requires only a node's own keypair and its configured group
keys. Path tables, clock synchronization, and session state are optional
enhancements, not prerequisites.

### Graceful Extensibility {#extensibility}

The protocol evolves without coordinated upgrades across a deployed network.
New features deploy incrementally, and older nodes degrade gracefully rather
than failing.

## Goals {#goals}

- **Public-key unicast.** A node's address is its public key, so knowing a
  node's key is enough to reach it, and a unicast packet is bound to the keys
  of the two nodes it travels between.
- **Group multicast.** A group is a shared symmetric key. Every node holding
  it can send to the group and receive what the group's members send.
- **Optional encryption.** Each packet is encrypted or left readable as its
  sender chooses, so one protocol carries both private traffic and traffic
  that has to stay in the clear.
- **End-to-end authentication.** A packet's authentication holds across every
  hop between its source and its destination. A repeater forwards a packet
  but cannot forge or alter its authenticated content.
- **Selective flooding and source routing.** A packet floods when no route is
  known and follows a source route when one is, and a flood can be limited by
  hop count, region, and signal quality.
- **Amateur radio operation.** Unencrypted modes that carry callsigns let
  UMSH operate legally on amateur frequencies.
- **Forward secrecy on request.** Conversations that want perfect forward
  secrecy get it, without imposing its cost on those that do not.
- **Replay protection without clocks.** Replays are detected without any node
  needing to know the time.

## Non-Goals {#non-goals}

- **High bandwidth.** LoRa data rates, typically 0.3–27 kbps, rule out
  real-time voice, video, and large file transfers.
- **Low latency.** Multi-hop flood delivery adds variable latency that suits
  neither interactive nor time-sensitive protocols.
- **Fragmentation in the MAC layer.** A protocol that needs more than one
  frame fragments and reassembles in its own payload.

## High-Level Design {#design}

### Layers {#layers}

UMSH defines a MAC layer that provides framing, addressing, encryption,
authentication, and hop-by-hop forwarding. On top of it, UMSH defines
application protocols for text messages, chat rooms, node management, and
more. The MAC layer treats payloads as opaque, so it carries UMSH's own
application protocols, third-party protocols such as CoAP, and any other
higher-layer content alike.

### Nodes and Identity {#identity}

A **node** is a logical endpoint, identified by a 32-byte Ed25519 public key.
That key is both the node's address and its credential. One physical device
may host several nodes, each with its own keypair.

Communicating with a node directly requires its public key, which can be
learned from:

- the beacons and advertisements that nodes broadcast
- URIs and QR codes shared out of band
- any packet whose sender includes its full key, as it does on first contact

Once a key is known, later packets identify the node by a compact hint
instead: three bytes in place of 32. Names, roles, capabilities, and location
travel at the application layer, and the MAC layer needs none of them.

### Unicast {#unicast}

A unicast packet is addressed to one node, and is authenticated or encrypted
with keys that the two nodes derive from key agreement between their keypairs.

### Groups {#groups}

A **group** is a shared symmetric key with two uses. Every node holding it can
send and receive the group's **multicast** traffic. It also carries **group
unicast**: a unicast secured with keys that only its two endpoints can
derive. When encrypted, its payload is readable only by its destination, and
its sender and destination are hidden from anyone outside the group.

### Forward Secrecy {#forward-secrecy}

Perfect forward secrecy is not built into the base protocol. Two nodes obtain
it by exchanging ephemeral node addresses, whose private keys are never stored
durably and are erased when the session ends. Compromising a long-term key
then exposes nothing from a completed session.

### Delivery {#delivery}

A packet reaches its destination directly within radio range, by flooding
across repeaters, along a source route through named repeaters, or by a
hybrid that source-routes to a region and floods from there. Each endpoint is
responsible for routing its own traffic to its destination.

### Devices and Hosts {#devices-and-hosts}

A **device** owns a radio and runs always-on firmware. A **host**, such as a
phone or computer, controls a device over a local link. The user's identity
lives on the host, not in the radio:

- a device is never given a private key that another device owns
- the host remains the authority for the user's identity, contacts, and
  application behavior
- a device acts on the user's behalf while disconnected only with state the
  host has provisioned for that purpose
